## Context

Authentication confirms a caller's identity, but BookWorm operations have
business-specific permissions, resource ownership rules, and administrative
requirements. These checks depend on the operation being requested and the
business resource involved. Centralizing them in an external identity provider
would separate authorization from the operations and data they govern, and
would make service-specific policy dependent on an external contract.

## Decision

The [[system|identity-provider]] authenticates users and supplies identity claims.
Every consuming BookWorm service validates the identity and applies its own
authorization policy, including endpoint permissions, resource ownership, and
administrative checks. A service must not treat a valid token or successful
authentication as sufficient authorization. It must evaluate the caller
against the rules for the specific operation and resource before allowing
that operation to proceed.

## Alternatives considered

- **Treat authentication or possession of a valid token as authorization.**
  This cannot account for resource ownership, operation-specific permissions,
  or administrative constraints, and would make a token's validity an
  overly broad grant.
- **Centralize BookWorm business authorization in the external provider.**
  This would require provider policy to track application-specific operations
  and domain resources, while separating authorization changes from the
  services that enforce and own those rules.

## Consequences

Authorization remains close to the business operation and the data it
protects. Services can enforce their own resource ownership and administrative
rules without requiring the provider to understand BookWorm domain semantics.
In exchange, each service must implement and maintain its checks consistently,
and must test protected operations independently of the sign-in flow. Shared
identity claims are an input to those checks, not a substitute for them;
services should avoid relying on provider claims to express mutable,
resource-specific business permissions. The separation also makes it possible
for a service to expose explicitly public or anonymous operations without
weakening authorization for protected ones.
