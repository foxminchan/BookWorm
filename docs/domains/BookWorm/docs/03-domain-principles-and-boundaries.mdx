## Guiding principles

BookWorm is designed as a bounded-context system, not as a single shared data
model. The domain is split so that ownership, semantics, and operational concerns
stay clear. This is what keeps the product understandable as it expands.

The following rules are the core operating model for the domain:

### 1. One context owns each source of truth

Every business concept has a home. Product facts live in [[domain|catalog]], the
mutable shopping intent lives in [[domain|shopping]], accepted purchases live in
[[domain|ordering]], and individual customer feedback lives in
[[domain|reviews]]. Platform capabilities such as notification delivery and
scheduled jobs support the business but do not own its state.

This rule prevents accidental cross-context updates and reduces the chance of
hidden data duplication.

### 2. Derived data is clearly marked and recoverable

Some information is intentionally copied or projected into other contexts. The
rating summary in Catalog is derived from Reviews. Search vectors are derived
from Catalog data. Cache entries are temporary optimization layers.

These are not independent systems of record. When they drift, they can be
rebuilt or replayed without rewriting the original source data.

### 3. Contracts cross contexts, not databases

Cross-context communication must happen through explicit commands, events,
queries, or synchronous service interfaces. The domain intentionally avoids
letting one context read or mutate another context's primary data store directly.

This is especially important in workflows such as:

- Basket to Order handoff
- Order status change propagation
- Review publication and Catalog rating projection
- Platform notifications triggered by business events

### 4. Operational work is separate from business ownership

Business correctness and operational execution are different responsibilities.
For example, a scheduled job may update a vector index or retry an email, but it
must not change the authoritative business state of an order or a book record.
This keeps auditing, debugging, and eventual consistency easier to reason about.

## Core invariants

These are the repeated expectations that hold across the domain:

- A basket is not an order.
- An accepted checkout creates a single order.
- Ordering owns the order lifecycle after acceptance.
- Catalog owns the canonical product and pricing metadata.
- Reviews owns the review record; Catalog owns only the derived summary.
- Platform notifications represent delivery work, not business state.
- Conversational systems may assist with access to information but do not own the
  primary records behind it.

## Ownership model by subdomain

The contexts in this table are [[domain|catalog]], [[domain|shopping]],
[[domain|ordering]], [[domain|reviews]], [[domain|conversational]], and
[[domain|platform]].

| Subdomain      | What it owns                                                   | What it does not own                       |
| -------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Catalog        | Product metadata, pricing, availability, and search projection | Individual review records or order state   |
| Shopping       | Basket state and pre-order intent                              | Accepted orders or final lifecycle state   |
| Ordering       | Order record and status transitions                            | Basket lifecycle or product master data    |
| Reviews        | Feedback records, ratings, and review history                  | Authoritative book metadata                |
| Conversational | Customer-facing interaction patterns and book support guidance | Booking, billing, and order-of-record data |
| Platform       | Notifications, scheduled jobs, and infrastructure support      | Business state of the domains it serves    |

## Change guidance for contributors

When designing or updating code, use the following checklist:

1. Identify the owning bounded context before touching a model.
2. Check whether the data is source-of-truth or derived.
3. Prefer explicit event or command boundaries over shared persistence.
4. Ensure the product experience still matches the domain contract.
5. Update the contract or documentation whenever the ownership model changes.

## Common anti-patterns to avoid

- Reading order status, review data, or basket state directly from another
  context's database.
- Treating a derived projection as the original source of truth.
- Mixing operational retry status with business state.
- Writing business logic into platform jobs or notification delivery flows.
- Interpreting an accepted email send as proof that a purchase or support action
  succeeded.

## Practical rule

If a change crosses a context boundary, ask three questions:

1. Which context owns the data?
2. What explicit contract carries the change?
3. Is the downstream side effect derived or authoritative?

If those answers are not clear, the design is not ready for implementation.
