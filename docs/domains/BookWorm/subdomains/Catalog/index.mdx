## 1. Context & Problem

Catalog is the authoritative **Core Domain** for the [[entity|book]]s BookWorm offers. It turns [[entity|publisher]] and [[entity|author]] information into a trusted, searchable product proposition used by [[domain|shopping]], [[domain|reviews]], and other customer-facing experiences.

Because the quality of product information directly drives customer discovery, conversion, and purchase accuracy, BookWorm needs a single domain responsible for defining what a book is, how it is classified, and what price and availability proposition is presented before checkout. Without explicit domain boundaries, product rules would fragment across shopping baskets, reviews, and order records.

### Domain scope and language

Catalog owns the product representation of a book and its descriptive relationships:

- [[entity|book]] is the central record and stable product identity.
- [[entity|author]], [[entity|publisher]], and [[entity|category]] provide discoverability and classification metadata.
- [[entity|book-author]] models the many-to-many relationship between books and authors.
- [[domain|reviews]] contributes rating information as a customer-feedback summary; Catalog does not own individual reviews.

Key domain distinctions:

- **Availability** describes whether a Catalog book can currently be offered—it is not a stock reservation owned by [[domain|shopping]] or a fulfilment promise owned by [[domain|ordering]].
- **Price** is part of the Catalog product proposition, though checkout must still verify current purchase-time validity before accepting an order.
- **Book identity** is shared through a stable identifier and published contracts, never by sharing Catalog's internal storage.

## 2. Business Value

- **Better product discovery and differentiation:** Meaningful [[entity|category]], [[entity|author]], and [[entity|publisher]] metadata—paired with differentiated search—helps shoppers find relevant books quickly.
- **Consistent customer expectations:** Shoppers see reliable pricing, availability, and rating summaries prior to checkout.
- **Autonomous downstream teams:** [[domain|shopping]] and [[domain|reviews]] can build customer experiences around stable [[entity|book]] identities and contracts without owning or altering Catalog business rules.
- **Independent availability and fast browsing:** Product discovery remains fast and available even when Reviews or Ordering experience load or downtime.
- **Clear accountability and auditability:** Business and engineering teams share one explicit contract for managing catalog information and tracing business-critical changes.

## 3. How It Works

Catalog organizes product management and distribution around four core capabilities and clear interaction boundaries with neighboring domains.

### Capability map

| Capability           | Responsibility                                                      | Strategic importance |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------- |
| Book management      | Create, update, retire, and retrieve book listings                  | Core                 |
| Classification       | Maintain categories, authors, and publishers                        | Core                 |
| Product proposition  | Expose price, availability, and rating summary for discovery        | Core                 |
| Contract publication | Provide stable API and integration semantics to downstream contexts | Enabling             |

### End-to-end interaction flow

1. An authorized catalog business user maintains product information through the Catalog service.
2. Catalog validates all business rules and stores the book along with its author, category, and publisher relationships.
3. Customer-facing experiences read published product representations for browsing and discovery.
4. [[domain|shopping]] verifies the current purchase-time proposition before accepting checkout.
5. Downstream domains keep their own independent models (such as [[entity|basket]]s, [[entity|feedback]], or historical order snapshots) and never modify Catalog state directly.

### Context boundaries

| Relationship                   | Direction | Integration intent                                                    |
| ------------------------------ | --------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Customer → Catalog             | Inbound   | Browse and inspect the product proposition                            |
| Catalog → Shopping             | Upstream  | Shopping consumes book, price, and availability information           |
| Catalog → Reviews              | Upstream  | Reviews uses Book identity and publishes feedback-derived context     |
| Catalog → [[domain\|ordering]] | Reference | Ordering may retain a purchase snapshot; it does not own Catalog data |

### Business invariants

- Every active book has a stable identity.
- Every book references valid category and publisher identities.
- A discount price cannot be negative or higher than the original price.
- An unavailable book must not be presented as available for a new purchase.
- Catalog updates must never silently rewrite an already accepted order.
- Reviews and Orders may keep historical snapshots, while Catalog remains the owner of the current product description.

## 4. Trade-offs & Lessons Learned

### Architecture principles

- **Bounded ownership:** Catalog owns book metadata and product presentation; [[domain|shopping]] owns [[entity|basket]] and checkout decisions; [[domain|ordering]] owns the order lifecycle.
- **Published language:** Downstream contexts consume explicit product contracts rather than importing internal Catalog models.
- **Read consistency vs. coupling:** Product discovery may use fast read projections, whereas purchase acceptance must verify the current live proposition to avoid stale prices or availability.
- **Contract-driven evolution:** Changes to published interfaces and messages must remain versioned and backward compatible for downstream consumers.
- **Traceable decisions:** Business-critical edits to price, availability, or product identity must remain auditable.

### Quality attributes and operational expectations

| Concern       | High-level expectation                                                          |
| ------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Availability  | Product discovery should remain available independently of Reviews and Ordering |
| Consistency   | The purchase path must not accept stale price or availability data              |
| Performance   | Browsing and product lookup should support low-latency reads                    |
| Evolvability  | Consumers should be insulated from internal persistence changes                 |
| Observability | Catalog changes and rejected updates should be traceable by product identity    |
| Security      | Catalog administration is restricted to authorized business users               |

### Architectural checkpoints to confirm

These solution-architecture decisions are intentionally flagged for ongoing confirmation rather than assumed:

- Whether downstream product data is delivered synchronously, via events, or through both patterns.
- Whether availability is mastered inside Catalog or integrated from a dedicated inventory capability.
- Whether rating summaries are refreshed synchronously or via a Reviews projection.
- What retention and audit policy governs historical price and availability snapshots.

### Value chain insight

Product search is where BookWorm differentiates, sitting closest to the shopper and furthest from commodity. Underlying data stores and compute infrastructure are treated as standard building blocks—bought or adopted rather than custom-built.
