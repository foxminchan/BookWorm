## Context

The Scheduler invokes recurring Catalog and Notification work, but it does not
own the business meaning of vector indexing, email delivery, or cleanup. If
scheduled jobs read or write downstream tables directly, Quartz becomes
coupled to domain schemas and a successful firing can be mistaken for a
successful business operation. The same separation is needed for security:
each target must authenticate and validate the Scheduler's request, and
should retain control over its own data, concurrency, retries, and outcome
reporting.

## Decision

The [[system|scheduler-system]] is an invocation and coordination boundary. Jobs publish
versioned integration contracts such as
[[event|ProcessVectorBatchIntegrationEvent]] over Kafka. The receiving system owns request validation, domain-level concurrency,
durable work state, retries, and the final business outcome.

The Scheduler must not read or write Catalog, Notification, or other domain
tables directly. A successful Quartz execution means that the Scheduler
completed its invocation path; it does not confirm that downstream processing
completed successfully. For example, the vector job invokes the authenticated
Catalog Ingestion endpoint, while email maintenance jobs publish requests for
Notification to select and process its own outbox records. The target's
response or message handling remains the boundary for its work.

## Consequences

- Downstream systems can evolve their storage and business rules without
  coupling them to Quartz tables.
- Scheduler operations must be correlated with target-system queues, outboxes,
  and processing metrics to determine business outcomes.
- Each receiving contract must define authentication, validation, and
  idempotency behavior.
- The architecture tolerates eventual consistency between a Scheduler firing
  and the target system's completed work.
- Scheduler history and health checks must be paired with target-side queue,
  outbox, or processing metrics to determine whether the requested work
  actually completed.
- Authentication failures, target-service outages, and message-publication
  failures remain observable as invocation failures, but do not grant
  Scheduler ownership of target-side recovery or data repair.
- Job payloads should carry only the invocation data the target contract
  needs; target systems determine eligible records and bounded work.

## Alternatives considered

### Query or mutate downstream databases from Quartz jobs

This could avoid target API or message hops, but would couple scheduler
deployment to domain schemas and bypass each target's validation,
authorization, and processing rules.

### Treat a successful firing as a successful business operation

For HTTP invocation this may only mean the endpoint returned; for Kafka it
may only mean publication succeeded. The target may still reject, defer, or
fail the work, so business success must be measured by the owning system.

### Make Scheduler own target retries and result state

This would duplicate target-specific work queues and recovery semantics in
Quartz. Scheduler is responsible for timing and invocation; the receiving
system remains responsible for durable work and its final outcome.
