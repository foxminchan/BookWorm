## 1. Context & Problem

BookWorm brings together distinct business capabilities—product catalog management, shopping baskets, order processing, customer reviews, conversational assistance, and shared platform operations. Without a clear structural strategy, growing e-commerce platforms tend to accumulate hidden cross-domain dependencies, shared databases, and tightly coupled business rules. When that happens, changing one rule risks breaking unrelated workflows, ownership becomes unclear, and teams lose the ability to release updates safely on their own.

This solution strategy establishes how BookWorm avoids those traps. It anchors architectural choices to the system's quality priorities in [[doc|arc42/01-introduction-and-goals/003-quality-goals]] and the system-wide boundaries in [[doc|arc42/02-architecture-constraints]].

## 2. Business Value

Aligning BookWorm's architecture to clear business boundaries delivers measurable value across product, delivery, and operations teams:

- **Trusted checkout and order accuracy:** Keeping book facts, basket state, and order lifecycles with their rightful owners protects critical business rules at checkout.
- **Safer, faster delivery:** When a change stays inside one capability and preserves existing agreements with other capabilities, teams can test and release it independently without full-platform coordination.
- **Lower cost of change:** Business rules can evolve inside a capability without dragging unrelated capabilities or transferring data ownership.
- **Clearer operational accountability:** When background schedules or customer notifications fail, operations teams can quickly identify which capability owns the failed step without confusing operational issues with business completion.

## 3. How It Works

BookWorm's strategy rests on five straightforward rules that guide every design and delivery decision:

1. **Organize by business capability, not technical layers.** Rather than grouping the platform around a shared business-logic tier, BookWorm divides responsibilities across [[domain|catalog]], [[domain|shopping]], [[domain|ordering]], [[domain|reviews]], [[domain|conversational]], and [[domain|platform]]. Each capability owns its own rules and data, making ownership and change impact immediately visible.
2. **Make every cross-capability dependency explicit.** Capabilities never reach directly into another capability's private data. When one capability needs information or wants to react to something that happened elsewhere, it uses documented interfaces and agreed business announcements with clear ownership.
3. **Keep shared platform capabilities supportive, not authoritative.** Common operational capabilities—such as sending notifications and running scheduled work—handle recurring infrastructure tasks without taking ownership of commerce rules or business outcomes.
4. **Build on a single unified platform foundation.** All teams work within the repository's established application platform rather than introducing incompatible runtimes or bespoke stacks, keeping tooling and operational practices consistent.
5. **Verify boundaries continuously as the platform evolves.** Teams pair functional and integration verification with automated boundary and contract checks, while keeping architectural documentation aligned with the live system so drift is caught early.

## 4. Trade-offs & Lessons Learned

- **Explicit integration effort vs. hidden coupling:** Separating capabilities and enforcing owned contracts requires more upfront interface design and coordination than calling across a shared layer, but it prevents cascading failures and makes long-term evolution predictable.
- **Coordinated migrations for breaking changes:** Independent release applies only when existing contracts remain compatible; whenever a breaking change is unavoidable, producers and consumers must plan a coordinated transition.
- **Unresolved operational targets:** This strategy intentionally avoids claiming numerical latency, availability, throughput, or recovery targets, and does not prescribe production deployment topologies or external service providers. Those commitments require explicit stakeholder agreement and are tracked alongside system constraints in [[doc|arc42/02-architecture-constraints]] and technical boundaries in [[doc|arc42/03-context-and-scope/003-technical-context]].

## 5. Technical Reference

### Platform & runtime decisions

- **Technology foundation:** The documented platform targets `.NET 11` and `C# 15`, with `.NET Aspire` supporting the cloud-native service model. New services and modules use this established setup rather than introducing an incompatible runtime.
- **Production topology & providers:** Production hosting topology and external providers are not specified by this strategy.

### Integration & contract mechanisms

- **Service contracts and integration events:** Cross-capability interactions use documented service interfaces and integration events with clear ownership.
- **Protocol Buffers:** Protocol Buffers are used for defined service interfaces; however, do not assume that every interaction uses that protocol or that an unspecified broker or transport is in place.

### Boundary & contract verification

- **Testing & checks:** Boundaries and contracts are validated continuously using business and integration tests, schema linting and compatibility checks for defined Protocol Buffer contracts, and architecture fitness checks.
- **Documentation parity:** Architecture documentation is kept aligned with the implementation so structural or contract drift remains visible.
