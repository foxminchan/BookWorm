## 1. Context & Problem

BookWorm's architecture affects a wide range of people—customers completing purchases, product and business leaders prioritizing capabilities, domain and platform teams building services, operations staff diagnosing incidents, and engineering leadership reviewing long-term coherence. When architecture documentation speaks only to developers, non-engineering roles struggle to see where business capabilities live, who owns an outcome, or how trade-offs affect their work.

Because the repository does not record individual contacts, this page captures **stakeholder roles** rather than named people, linking each role's concerns to the architecture chapters built to answer them.

## 2. Business Value

- **Faster answers for every role**: Directs product, business, delivery, platform, operations, and architecture readers straight to the information relevant to their decisions.
- **Clear team boundaries**: Reinforces the distinction between business-owning domain teams and enabling infrastructure teams (such as [[team|platform-team]]), preventing accidental ownership drift.
- **Better operational triage**: Gives support and operations teams explicit capability boundaries and observable outcomes so delayed or failed work reaches the right owner quickly.
- **Aligned prioritization**: Grounds business and technical discussions in shared requirements and quality scenarios rather than competing assumptions.

## 3. How It Works

Each stakeholder group interacts with BookWorm from a distinct perspective, summarized below:

| Stakeholder role                        | Expectations and concerns                                                                                              | What they need from the architecture                                                                 |
| --------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Customers and other platform users      | Discover books, make informed purchases, and receive understandable outcomes from checkout and supporting experiences. | A coherent journey with trustworthy product information and clear ownership of purchase outcomes.    |
| Product and business stakeholders       | Understand which capabilities support business goals and where a change belongs.                                       | A concise view of system purpose, essential capabilities, limitations, and quality priorities.       |
| Domain and service teams                | Build and change capabilities without silently taking over another context's data or behavior.                         | Clear ownership boundaries, integration contracts, and change-impact information.                    |
| Platform and infrastructure teams       | Provide shared technical capabilities without becoming owners of business rules (see [[team\|platform-team]]).         | The responsibilities, constraints, and expected interactions of cross-cutting services.              |
| Operations and support teams            | Diagnose failed or delayed processing and determine which team or capability is responsible.                           | A useful system boundary, explicit operational responsibilities, and observable processing outcomes. |
| Architecture and engineering leadership | Evaluate whether the decomposition and trade-offs remain coherent as BookWorm evolves.                                 | The drivers, constraints, prioritized quality scenarios, and rationale for architectural direction.  |

## 4. Trade-offs & Lessons Learned

- **Role catalog vs. governance directory**: Documenting stakeholder roles instead of named individuals keeps the architecture documentation stable as team membership changes, but means this page is a working overview rather than a record of named approvers or formal governance.
- **Next step for governance**: Product and architecture owners should record named contacts, decision rights, and formal review responsibilities once those governance structures are established.
- **Multi-audience clarity**: Separating high-level business purpose and quality priorities from implementation details ensures non-coding stakeholders can validate architectural direction without wading through service internals.
